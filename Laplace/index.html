Laplace Transforms, and why process control lives in the s-domain

Differential equations describe the plant. Algebra is what you can actually reason about. The Laplace transform is the bridge — turn d/dt into multiplication by s, solve the algebra, and read stability, offset and speed of response straight off the result. Worked textbook examples below, each with a live plot.

01The one trick that matters

Every derivative becomes a multiplication. For a variable at steady state initially (y(0) = 0 in deviation form):

ℒ{ dy/dt } = s·Y(s)     ℒ{ d²y/dt² } = s²·Y(s)     ℒ{ ∫y dt } = Y(s)/s

So a linear ODE in time becomes a polynomial equation in s. Rearranged as output over input, that ratio is the transfer function:

G(s) = Y(s) / U(s)

Deviation variables come first

Before transforming anything, textbooks (Seborg; Coughanowr; Marlin) subtract the steady state:

y′ = y − ȳ      u′ = u − ū   ⟹   y′(0) = 0

This kills the initial-condition terms and the constants, so the transfer function is clean. It also means the transfer function describes changes around an operating point — which is exactly what a controller manipulates.

03Example 1 — first-order process (stirred tank / thermowell)

Textbook · Seborg Ch.5 · Coughanowr Ch.4

Energy balance on a well-mixed heated tank, or a thermocouple in a thermowell, reduces to:

τ · dy′/dt + y′ = K · u′

Transform term by term with y′(0) = 0:

τ s Y(s) + Y(s) = K U(s)  ⟹  G(s) = K / (τs + 1)

Apply a step of size M, so U(s) = M/s:

Y(s) = KM / [ s(τs + 1) ]  ⟹  y(t) = KM (1 − e−t/τ)

Read it off: final value KM (FVT confirms: s·Y(s) → KM as s→0), 63.2% of the change at t = τ, and 98% done by 4τ. That's your whole step test interpretation.

04Example 2 — second order, and where oscillation comes from

Textbook · two tanks in series · Seborg Ch.5

Two non-interacting first-order stages multiply in the s-domain — that is the real payoff of Laplace. Series elements just cascade:

G(s) = K/(τ₁s+1) · 1/(τ₂s+1)

Written in standard second-order form:

G(s) = K / ( τ²s² + 2ζτ s + 1 )

The character of the response is decided entirely by the poles, the roots of the denominator:

  • ζ > 1 — two real poles, overdamped, sluggish, no overshoot. Tanks in series.
  • ζ = 1 — critically damped, fastest without overshoot.
  • 0 < ζ < 1 — complex conjugate poles, underdamped: overshoot and ringing. Typical of a closed loop tuned aggressively.
  • ζ ≤ 0 — poles on or right of the imaginary axis: sustained or growing oscillation. Unstable.

Classic results for underdamped step response — overshoot OS = exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)), decay ratio = OS², period T = 2πτ/√(1−ζ²).

05Example 3 — dead time, the reason loops go unstable

FOPDT · the model behind every PID tuning rule

Transport delay through a pipe shifts the signal in time. Laplace handles it with one exponential factor:

G(s) = K e−θs / (τs + 1)

Three parameters — gain K, time constant τ, dead time θ — fitted from a single bump test. Cohen–Coon, IMC and Ziegler–Nichols tuning correlations all take exactly these three numbers as input, and the ratio θ/τ is the difficulty index: below 0.2 the loop is easy, above 1.0 you are fighting it.

Why it destabilises: e−θs has magnitude 1 but contributes phase lag −ωθ that grows without bound with frequency. Gain is untouched, phase margin is eaten alive.

06Example 4 — closing the loop, and proving PI removes offset

Block algebra · servo & regulator response

With controller Gc, process Gp, the closed loop collapses to one formula because everything is algebra now:

Y(s)/Ysp(s) = GcGp / (1 + GcGp)

The denominator set to zero — 1 + GcGp = 0 — is the characteristic equation. Its roots are the closed-loop poles. Any root with a positive real part means an unstable loop. That is the whole of classical stability analysis.

Proportional-only leaves offset

With Gc = Kc and Gp = K/(τs+1), a unit setpoint step gives, by Final Value Theorem:

y(∞) = KcK / (1 + KcK)  <  1  ⟹  offset = 1/(1 + KcK)

Adding integral action kills it

With Gc = Kc(1 + 1/τIs), the controller contributes a pole at the origin. Redo the FVT and the offset term vanishes identically:

y(∞) = 1    for any Kc, τI that keeps the loop stable

No simulation needed — two lines of algebra in the s-domain prove a result you would otherwise have to trust from experience.

07The workflow, condensed

  • Write the balance — mass, energy, momentum. Linearise around the operating point if needed.
  • Convert to deviation variables so all initial conditions are zero.
  • Laplace transform. Derivatives become s, integrals become 1/s, delays become e−θs.
  • Rearrange into G(s) = Y(s)/U(s). Combine blocks by multiplying in series, adding in parallel.
  • Form 1 + GcGp = 0 and check the pole locations for stability.
  • Use the Final Value Theorem for offset and steady-state gain; invert only when you need the full time trace.
